# [White Is a New Shade of Blue Carbon: A Case Study of a Traditional Salt Production Pond That is a Net Carbon Sink](https://doi.org/10.1029/2025jg009016)

**Authors:** Ana Alexandre, Carmen B. de los Santos, J. Herrero et al.
**Venue:** JGR Biogeosciences · **Date:** 2026-05-01 · **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1029/2025jg009016
**Score:** 8/10<sup> top pick</sup>
**Source:** abstract

## TL;DR

A traditional salt production saltern is a net carbon sink at −35.0 g CO₂-eq m⁻² yr⁻¹, qualifying it as "white carbon" analogous to blue carbon ecosystems.

## Summary

- **Problem:** Carbon budgets of traditional salt pans (salterns) are uncharacterized; it is unknown whether the salt industry constitutes a carbon sink or source compared to other coastal aquaculture industries.
- **Method:** Diel, seasonal, and spatial measurements of water-air CO₂ and CH₄ fluxes, plus sediment organic carbon (OC) stock and burial rate, in one traditional saltern. Environmental drivers (temperature, salinity, dissolved O₂, pH, wind) assessed via regression.
- **Key result:** Net carbon budget −35.0 g CO₂-eq m⁻² yr⁻¹ (−1.49 Mg CO₂-eq yr⁻¹ for full saltern area). Sediment OC stock averaged 11.9 ± 2.2 Mg OC ha⁻¹; burial rate 0.011 ± 0.004 Mg OC ha⁻¹ yr⁻¹. CH₄ emitted year-round (temperature explained 29% of variance); CO₂ was a sink in summer/autumn/winter but a source in spring. O₂ and pH explained 66% of CO₂ flux variance.
- **Why it matters:** Salt pans could represent an overlooked coastal carbon sink ("white carbon"), contrasting with fish/shrimp aquaculture which is typically a net source; findings motivate inclusion of salterns in coastal blue carbon inventories.
- **Caveats:** Single saltern in one geographic location; OC burial rates are low and CH₄ warming potential offsets some CO₂ sequestration; broader generalization requires measurements across a wider range of saltern types and climates.
