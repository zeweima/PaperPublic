# [Network Connectivity and Local Hydrology Drive DOM Composition in a Nonperennial Stream](https://doi.org/10.1029/2025jg009568)

**Authors:** Sarah M. Flynn, Rebecca L. Hale, Erin Seybold et al.
**Venue:** JGR Biogeosciences · **Date:** 2026-05-01 · **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1029/2025jg009568
**Score:** 9/10 <sup>top pick</sup>
**Source:** abstract

## TL;DR

In a nonperennial prairie stream, network-scale hydrologic connectivity and local water persistence jointly control whether DOM is humic/terrestrial or autochthonous/microbially processed.

## Summary

- **Problem:** How hydrologic connectivity at network vs. local scales shapes dissolved organic matter (DOM) composition in nonperennial streams remains poorly understood, limiting predictions of carbon export and bioavailability under shifting flow regimes.
- **Method:** 3 years of hydrologic monitoring and fluorescence spectroscopy (EEM-PARAFAC implied) of surface water across a nonperennial prairie stream network; principal component analyses to partition DOM variance by site and hydrologic phase.
- **Key result:** Humic, terrestrially derived DOM components explained the most variance overall and were highest at headwater sites. Autochthonous DOM was relatively greater at sites with persistent local surface water and flowing conditions. High network connectivity diluted local autochthonous signals via mixing and transport; low or transitional flows enhanced retention, autochthonous production, and microbial processing.
- **Why it matters:** Hydrologic fragmentation — increasingly common under climate change — shifts DOM from a transported terrestrial signal toward locally produced, potentially more bioavailable material, altering downstream carbon cycling and aquatic metabolism.
- **Caveats:** Single stream network (prairie biome); fluorescence proxies do not fully resolve DOM molecular composition; extrapolation to other climate regions or stream types requires caution.
