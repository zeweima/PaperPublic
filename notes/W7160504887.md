# [Unprecedented Shifts in Hydrology Are Emerging Across California's Critical Basins: An Evaluation From 0.5 to 3.5°C](https://doi.org/10.1029/2025wr041338)

**Authors:** B. Bass, L. Su, D. Pierce et al.
**Venue:** Water Resources Research · **Date:** 2026-05-01 · **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1029/2025wr041338
**Score:** 9/10<sup> top pick</sup>
**Source:** abstract

## TL;DR

California's critical water supply basins show statistically significant snow loss and extreme runoff intensification at near-term warming levels already observable in reanalysis data.

## Summary

- **Problem:** No prior study had identified the specific warming thresholds at which hydroclimate metrics shift significantly across California's major water supply basins, leaving managers without clear signals for when to adapt.
- **Method:** 19 ESMs downscaled to 3-km resolution via statistical-dynamical approach for California's 5th Climate Assessment; two calibrated hydrology models run across warming levels from 0.5 to 3.5°C; analysis covers Klamath, Northern Sierra Nevada, and other basins stratified by elevation (intermediate ~1,315–1,455 m).
- **Key result:** Klamath and Northern Sierra Nevada basins show statistically significant decreases in snowfall, peak snowpack, summer snowmelt, and runoff at present-day to near-term warming levels (~0.5–1°C). Intermediate-elevation basins (1,315–1,455 m) emerge earliest; lower and higher elevations lag. Multiple basins already show significant increases in 5- to 100-year runoff intensities due to rain-on-snow and rain replacement of snow.
- **Why it matters:** Near-term emergence signals confirmed in reanalysis data indicate California has already entered a structurally different hydroclimate regime; findings provide actionable timing guidance for when water management adaptation is needed, not just what will change.
- **Caveats:** Statistical-dynamical downscaling may not fully resolve orographic precipitation processes; two hydrology models may not bracket full uncertainty in evapotranspiration or groundwater feedbacks; focus on California limits direct generalizability to other snowmelt-dependent systems.
