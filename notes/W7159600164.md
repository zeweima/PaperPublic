# [Iron supply from the Oregon margin to the ocean dominated by hypoxia-dependent particles](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2532929123)

**Authors:** Alexis E. Floback, Anh L. D. Pham, Pierre Damien et al.
**Venue:** PNAS · **Date:** 2026-04-30 · **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2532929123
**Score:** 9/10<sup> top pick</sup>
**Source:** abstract

## TL;DR

Cross-shelf Fe export from the Oregon margin is dominated by particulate Fe in hypoxia-driven benthic nepheloid plumes, not dissolved Fe, with basin-scale reach into HNLC regions.

## Summary

- **Problem:** Continental margins are both sources and sinks of Fe; dissolved Fe released from reducing sediments is mostly retained on-shelf by oxidative scavenging ("Fe trapping"), yet the dominant pathway and mechanism for cross-shelf Fe export remains unclear.
- **Method:** Field observations from the Oregon shelf (2021) combined with a physical-biogeochemical model incorporating oxygen-dependent particle sources and realistic settling velocities; inverse model simulations to trace plume extent offshore.
- **Key result:** Cross-shelf export is overwhelmingly particulate, not dissolved. Particles accumulate in a 30 m thick benthic nepheloid layer within hypoxic zones and form persistent plumes extending beyond the shelf-slope break; both nonlithogenic and lithogenic particulate Fe components are present, implicating hypoxia-driven changes in particle buoyancy and resuspension. Slope-settling particles generate large Fe plumes extending 200–2,000 m depth westward; inverse model shows plumes outcrop in Fe-limited high-nutrient low-chlorophyll (HNLC) regions.
- **Why it matters:** Hypoxia-enhanced particle buoyancy is a previously unrecognized Fe mobilization mechanism that, combined with classical redox processes, links coastal deoxygenation to basin-scale Fe supply; expanding OMZs could amplify Fe delivery to HNLC waters.
- **Caveats:** Single-year (2021) field campaign on one margin; generalizability to other hypoxic margins untested; model particle buoyancy parameterization relies on organic geochemistry advances not fully constrained observationally.
