# [Low Climate Benefit of Nordic Coastal Marshes: Site Conditions Outweigh Grazing Effects and Shape Trade‐Offs Between Carbon Storage and Its Stability](https://doi.org/10.1111/gcb.70889)

**Authors:** Carmen Leiva-Dueñas, Gary Thomas Banta, Christoffer Boström et al.
**Venue:** Global Change Biology · **Date:** 2026-05-01 · **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1111/gcb.70889
**Score:** 8/10<sup> top pick</sup>
**Source:** abstract

## TL;DR

Nordic coastal marshes offer limited net climate benefit because site conditions and cattle grazing emissions offset organic carbon sequestration and CH₄ reductions.

## Summary

- **Problem:** Coastal marshes are promoted as blue carbon sinks, but their actual greenhouse gas balance across Nordic sites — accounting for methane emissions, grazing management, and soil variability — is poorly constrained.
- **Method:** Multi-site study across Danish and Finnish coastal marshes comparing grazed vs. ungrazed plots; measured soil organic carbon (OC) stocks, OC accumulation rates, methane fluxes, and estimated GHG emissions from on-site cattle.
- **Key result:** Methane emissions weakened the carbon sink by 32% in Finland and 68% in Denmark in ungrazed marshes. Grazing reduced methane fluxes significantly but did not increase OC stocks or accumulation rates. GHG emissions from on-site cattle (even at low intensity) exceeded the climate benefit of the marshes. Danish marshes had highest but most vulnerable OC stocks (faster turnover, labile compounds, coarser soils).
- **Why it matters:** Challenges the narrative that coastal marsh management through grazing improves climate outcomes; site-specific soil conditions (grain size, decomposability) dominate carbon stability over management effects, suggesting Nordic blue carbon potential is limited.
- **Caveats:** Abstract is truncated — full quantification of OC stocks and accumulation rates across all sites unclear; findings may not extend beyond Nordic/temperate marshes; cattle emission estimates depend on grazing intensity assumptions.
